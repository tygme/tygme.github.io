Where expert judgment was the only way through

Six problems that turned on judgment no one had written down, and how MindModeler captured it where today’s AI could not.

Six hard problemsMining furnace and database tunersTry the furnace model

Hard problems

Six problems today’s AI could not reach

Each one turned on judgment that was never written down. MindModeler, the expert-modeling tool in his TG-Engine collection of AI tools, captured it as a working model. The cases come from his work and from his 2001 dissertation, where the tool is called the ADPME. It was also tried with an internist.

NASA Lewis Research Center · 1989

The structures expert

The problem
"There are experts who can look at a mechanical structure or its design and pronounce whether it is an optimal design or not. How can we capture and replicate that expertise?" asked the chief scientist for structures. Supercomputers had run for weeks in search of optimal designs.
What MindModeler did
A neural network learned one expert's judgment from just ten ten-bar trusses: the surface that spans the space of optimal solutions. His answers checked out against analytical optimization, and the work extended to a hundred-bar box wing.
Why today’s AI could not
No document holds one expert's sense of what is optimal, and ten examples are far too few for today's models to learn from.
In business terms
A career’s worth of design judgment, reproduced from ten examples, on problems where supercomputers had run for weeks.

A Fortune 50 consulting firm · dissertation, 2001

Three award-winning database tuners

The problem
Asked how he tunes, one expert sat for two hours with a blank page, an hour doodling and another hour drawing a cause map. Tuning a production database is exponential: no amount of hardware solves it. The key is the human expert.
What MindModeler did
Each tuner's knowledge was captured in under an hour. In production: 6 hours cut to 32 minutes; 8 hours to 2 minutes 16 seconds; a 31-minute process to under 3½ minutes; an interactive query from 90 seconds to 1. Of 74 database professionals shown the models, 70 said no existing tool could help them and four were unsure; all 74 said this one would.
Why today’s AI could not
We asked Grok, DeepSeek and Gemini how to tune it. All three gave the textbook order, SQL first, and ranked the tuners’ winning moves last. One tuner believed in SQL first too, until her own model showed she rarely did it. See the test.
In business terms
Two long batch jobs became coffee-break jobs: 14 hours of run time cut to about 34 minutes, roughly 13½ hours given back every time they run, and people stopped waiting 90 seconds for an answer.

A Fortune 500 corporation

The retiring formulation scientist

The problem
A Ph.D. with more than 35 years of specialty formulations was leaving. Three person-years built a rule-based expert system that could not produce acceptable formulations. A purchased repository could not create new ones. His lab notebooks were digitized.
What MindModeler did
MindModeler mapped a formulation scientist’s judgment as a working model of causes and effects: the kind of knowledge the notebooks and the rules had missed.
Why today’s AI could not
Digitizing the notebooks is exactly what today's document search (RAG) does. It made them faster to search. It did not make a single new formulation.
In business terms
What three person-years of rule-writing could not keep: how the expert creates new formulations, kept before he walked out the door.

U.S. Air Force · Wright Laboratory

The master artisans

The problem
Craftsmen with decades on the job. Programs in LISP and CLOS captured little more than the rules in books and manuals. Interviews, narratives and diaries said little about how they made their decisions. "Nothing has worked very well," the dissertation records.
What MindModeler did
Memory-driven design that the Air Force published as a Success Story: casting-pattern design cut from two weeks to under two days. The artisans' design judgment was mapped, from energy losses to rate of flow.
Why today’s AI could not
The skill was never written down. Rules, interviews and transcripts, the raw material of today's AI, return only the manual.
In business terms
Casting patterns in under two days instead of two weeks. In the same Wright Laboratory rapid-prototyping program, $516 thousand was saved on one B-52 part before it was ever made, for a bomber the Air Force plans to fly until 2050.

Fortune 100 and multinational executives

The executive who could not explain

A senior executive reached a firm conclusion but could not show how: not with premises, not with cause maps, not with the help of like-minded colleagues. Another needed the best mix of presentation styles and content for each negotiation, under time limits.

The retiring furnace engineer

The problem
More than 30 years balancing NOx, CO and SO₂ at a furnace that was "an art form to operate," with the option to retire.
What MindModeler did
His model surfaced a relationship he had never put into words. His note: "Ah-ha: never gave this info."

We asked Grok, DeepSeek and ChatGPT the same question. None found any of the model's four findings in full, and all three said the opposite on two. See the test.

Why these stay out of reach for today’s AI

  1. It was never written down. Chatbots and document search learn from the record. This knowledge was not in it.
  2. There are only a few examples. Ten trusses, ten to twenty episodes. Today’s models need thousands, or billions of words.
  3. The expert cannot explain it. Ask, and experts recite the textbook. MindModeler learns from how they react to real cases, then shows them what they know.

Case study

Thirty years of furnace judgment, made explicit

A large mining company · MindModeler · 2002

Situation

A senior process engineer with more than 30 years' experience, eligible to retire, ran a low-pressure coal-burning furnace. He balanced SO₂, NOₓ and CO limits against fuel, furnace and air flows. Every mine differed in ore, fuel and equipment, which made operating it an art. None of that judgment was written down.

Approach

He named what he could control and what he could not, and described how each factor moved the others. That became a semantic model. His recollections of real episodes became an episodic model. MindModeler's clustering, what-if analysis and weighted goal-seeking then turned both into working tools.

What the model showed

  • Primary air temperature was the single most important variable.
  • NOₓ followed a complex parabolic surface, high when both heat inputs were high and when both were low.
  • For CO, the share of heat replaced, not total heat input, was the culprit.
  • To reduce NOₓ, increase fired pellets.

One finding drew this note from the expert: Ah-ha: never gave this info. The model had surfaced knowledge he had never put into words.

Second case

A production database, from hours to minutes

A production Oracle database · MindModeler episodic memory · three tuning phases

The experts. The knowledge came from three award-winning database tuners at a Fortune 50 consulting firm; this is real-life wicked tuning problem no. 2 in his dissertation. Like the mining engineer and the retiring Ferro scientist, they were a textbook case of experiential learning: asked how they worked, they recited the rules and standard operating procedures, yet at the keyboard they did something different, guided by gut feel, intuition, know-how and judgment, often without realizing they were departing from the rules. In the words of his patent, a decision-maker is not necessarily aware of how he arrives at a particular decision and of his own tacit knowledge.

The problem. Two processes in a production database took six and eight hours to run. MindModeler organized every tuning attempt as an episode, linked it to the factors involved (memory size, partitioned tables, SQL WHERE clauses, time, space and the administrator's workload) and showed which changes paid off.

Process 1 ran 91% faster and process 2 99.5% faster. The model also showed a trade-off the written rules missed: forcing the optimizer with hints made the administrator's workload climb steeply, while improving the queries themselves kept it nearly flat.

Grokdatabase question · fresh chat · November 7, 2026

Our commentary: the textbook hierarchy stated plainly: rewrite the SQL first, avoid rule-based hints. It does match the workload trade-off the tuners’ models found.

Rewrite the SQL first (after analyzing the statements and their execution plans). This is the highest-leverage, most targeted step in the classic Oracle performance methodology and the right starting point among the listed options.Recommended order and rationaleOracle’s long-standing performance method (still reflected in modern guidance) prioritizes application/SQL changes before instance or physical changes, because they usually deliver the largest gains with the least systemic side effects:
Rewrite the SQL — Examine the batch SQL (via trace, AWR/ASH, SQL Tuning Advisor, execution plans, and statistics). Fix common problems such as missing join predicates, functions on columns that prevent index use, unnecessary sorts/joins, Cartesian products, poor driving tables, or statements that can be simplified/broken up. This addresses the root cause directly.
Add or change indexes (only after the SQL is understood) — Create or modify indexes for the critical access paths the rewritten (or original) SQL needs. Prefer invisible indexes for testing when possible.
Change memory settings (sort/hash area sizes or PGA) — Apply mainly at the session level for the batch jobs if plans show large sorts or hash joins spilling to temp. Prefer automatic PGA management with session overrides over system-wide changes.
Reorganize tables — Only if diagnostics show clear problems (excessive migrated/chained rows, severe fragmentation affecting full scans or range scans). This is lower priority and often temporary.
Hints to force rule-based optimization (RBO) — Avoid. The RULE hint is unsupported, ignores statistics, does not understand modern features, and produces non-adaptive plans. Oracle has long recommended against it.

Scope and risk: Prefer statement- or session-level changes over system-wide ones. A memory or index change that helps the batch can hurt concurrent OLTP. SQL rewrites and carefully chosen indexes are more targeted.
Adaptivity vs. stability: Cost-based optimization with good statistics adapts as data changes. Hard-coded RBO or aggressive hints do not; they create future work. Modern alternatives (SQL Plan Management, profiles, baselines) are preferable to raw hints when plan control is needed.

DeepSeekdatabase question · fresh chat · November 7, 2026

Our commentary: it calls SQL-first “the consensus among Oracle performance experts.” That is exactly the consensus the award-winning tuners departed from to get their results.

When two batch processes are stretching from 6 and 8 hours, the instinct to reach for a quick technical lever—a new index, a memory bump, or a hint—is strong. However, in an Oracle production environment, the **order in which you apply these changes determines whether you solve the problem or simply move the bottleneck while creating long-term maintenance debt for the DBA team.**

The consensus among Oracle performance experts is clear: **start with the SQL and the data access paths. Leave hints and physical reorganization as last resorts.** Here is the recommended priority, the long-term workload implications for the DBA, and the trade-offs an expert must weigh.

### 🥇 1. First: SQL Rewrite (and Query Optimization)
This should almost always be the first step. Batch processes often suffer from fundamental design issues: row-by-row processing (RBAR), Cartesian joins causing row explosion, or literal-heavy SQL that prevents cursor reuse.

- **Impact on DBA Workload Over Time:** **High initial effort, low ongoing maintenance.** A DBA or developer must invest significant time profiling the workload, reading execution plans, and refactoring the code. However, once the SQL is efficient, it stays efficient. It reduces the need for emergency interventions and makes execution plans more stable, freeing the DBA from constant firefighting.
- **Key Trade-off:** **Time-to-value vs. Durability.** It is the most time-consuming option upfront and requires deep application knowledge, but it addresses the root cause. It is far more durable than any parameter or hint because it improves the logic itself, not just the environment it runs in.

### 🥈 2. Second: Add or Change Indexes
Indexing should be tightly coupled with the SQL rewrite. The goal is not to index every column, but to **redesign indexes around the actual access patterns** revealed by the SQL's `WHERE`, `JOIN`, and `ORDER BY` clauses. A common mistake is adding indexes blindly without considering how the SQL will use them.

- **Impact on DBA Workload Over Time:** **Moderate.** Creating an index is quick, but it introduces ongoing responsibilities: monitoring index usage, rebuilding or coalescing fragmented indexes, and ensuring statistics are fresh. Every new index adds overhead to DML operations (inserts/updates/deletes), which can degrade other parts of the system if not chosen carefully.
- **Key Trade-off:** **Read performance vs. Write performance and Storage.** Indexes dramatically speed up data retrieval for batch reports, but they slow down data modification and consume additional disk space. The DBA must monitor for **unnecessary or unused indexes** that only add overhead.

### 🥉 3. Third: Memory Settings (Sort/Hash Area Sizes)
Tuning PGA memory (e.g., `PGA_AGGREGATE_TARGET`) or manual work area sizes can provide a quick performance boost for operations that are spilling to disk (multi-pass executions). However, this should be done **after** the SQL and indexes are sane; otherwise, you are just giving more memory to inefficient operations.

- **Impact on DBA Workload Over Time:** **Low, if using automatic management.** Modern Oracle versions use automatic PGA management, which dynamically adjusts work areas based on workload, greatly simplifying the DBA's job. Manual tuning (e.g., setting `SORT_AREA_SIZE`) is difficult and error-prone because the optimal size changes with the workload. If automatic management is used, the ongoing workload is minimal.
- **Key Trade-off:** **Immediate speed vs. Resource Consumption.** Bigger work areas significantly improve the performance of a specific sort or hash join, but at the cost of higher memory consumption. Over-allocating memory can starve other processes or lead to swapping. The goal is to have most operations run in **optimal** or **one-pass** size, avoiding multi-pass executions which are exponentially slower.

### 🚫 4. Last Resort: Hints (Especially Rule-Based Optimization)
Using hints, particularly forcing **Rule-Based Optimization (RBO)** , is generally discouraged. The RBO has been deprecated since Oracle 10g/11g and does not support modern features like partitioning. Even for cost-based hints, Oracle's own documentation warns that **hints introduce extra code to manage, check, and control**, and changes in the database or host environment can make them obsolete or even harmful.

- **Impact on DBA Workload Over Time:** **High and Fragile.** Every hint is a hard-coded directive that must be revisited with every database upgrade, statistics change, or data volume shift. Hints can become "stale" and cause the optimizer to make terrible choices later. They create a hidden maintenance burden that accumulates over time.
- **Key Trade-off:** **Immediate control vs. Long-term instability.** A hint can force a specific plan today, but it masks the underlying problem (often bad statistics or poorly written SQL) and creates a fragile system that is hard to adapt. **Use hints only as a temporary stop-gap or when a specific, well-understood edge case requires them, not as a primary tuning strategy.**

### ⏸️ 5. Rarely First: Reorganize Tables
Table reorganization (e.g., `ALTER TABLE MOVE`, `SHRINK SPACE`, or online redefinition) is often performed unnecessarily. It is only helpful for performance if you have **proven, significant fragmentation** (e.g., extensive row chaining/migration) that is causing full table scans to read far more blocks than necessary.

- **Impact on DBA Workload Over Time:** **High initial effort and risk.** Reorganization can generate massive redo/archive logs, cause locking, and require downtime or complex online redefinition procedures. It is a heavy operational task that does not address the root cause of slow SQL; the table will simply fragment again over time.
- **Key Trade-off:** **Potential I/O improvement vs. Operational Risk.** The gain is usually marginal unless fragmentation is extreme. The risk of application impact, log generation, and the sheer DBA time required make it one of the **last** things to try. DBAs should **verify fragmentation is truly impacting performance** before rebuilding.

### 💎 Summary: The Expert's Decision Framework
An expert DBA does not treat these as interchangeable levers. They follow a diagnostic hierarchy:

1.  **Measure first.** Use AWR/ASH to identify the actual top SQL by elapsed time and wait events—do not guess.
2.  **Fix the SQL logic.** Rewrite the most expensive statements to be set-based and efficient.
3.  **Align indexes.** Create or adjust indexes to match the new access patterns.
4.  **Right-size memory.** Ensure PGA is adequate (ideally via automatic management) to support the new plans without spilling to disk.
5.  **Avoid hints.** Use SQL Plan Baselines or profiles instead of hints to stabilize plans if necessary, as they are more manageable.
6.  **Reorganize only on evidence.** Do it only if metrics prove fragmentation is the cause of excessive I/O.

This sequence delivers the largest, most durable performance gain (often 90%+ reductions in runtime) while **minimizing the DBA's long-term maintenance burden** and avoiding the creation of a fragile, hint-dependent system.
Geminidatabase question · fresh chat · November 7, 2026

Our commentary: the most absolute of the three: rewriting the SQL “must always come first,” and rule-based hints are a “strict anti-pattern.” The tuners’ biggest documented wins came from the moves it rules out.

Rewriting the SQL must always come first when tuning long-running batch processes.
The most effective way to reduce a 6- or 8-hour execution time is to eliminate unnecessary work at the source. Badly written SQL forces the database engine into massive logical I/O, Cartesian joins, or unnecessary row-by-row processing (cursor loops) that no amount of memory or indexing can fully fix. By addressing the SQL first, you attack the root cause rather than masking the symptoms with infrastructure changes.
Once the code is verified to be asking the database for the right data in the most efficient way, you can evaluate the physical and architectural levers.

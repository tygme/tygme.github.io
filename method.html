The leak

Why today's AI cannot plug the hole

Every mainstream AI approach learns from a record: documents, tickets, transcripts, past data. Each is blind to what the record leaves out. Your best people's judgment is exactly what the record leaves out, so it keeps leaking out through retirements while the AI spend goes up.

ApproachWhat it learns fromWhat it misses

Fine-tuningA frozen snapshot of past dataThe expert keeps learning. The snapshot does not.

Interviews and rule-based expert systemsWhat the expert can sayThe expert recites the textbook. One Fortune 500 manufacturer spent three man-years on a rule system for a retiring scientist that "failed to generate acceptable custom formulations."

This describes how these approaches work in general, not any named product. Newer tools that mine tickets and transcripts get closer to real behavior, but they still learn from a record. Accenture's authors in California Management Review (March 2026) reach the same point: the differentiator for agentic AI is not the data or even the models, but the tacit knowledge in people's judgment.

Why it is not an easy task

Watch an expert, and the expert performs for you

This is the Hawthorne effect, named for productivity studies at Western Electric's Hawthorne Plant in the 1920s and early 1930s: people change their behavior when they know they are being watched. Those original experiments have since been heavily criticized, and researchers now prefer the term "research participation effects," but the practical warning stands. Observation changes behavior, and interviews fare no better, because asked how they work, experts describe the textbook.

An old problem

Plato saw it 2,400 years ago

In the Meno, a student asks how anyone can search for something they do not already know. Socrates answers by drawing geometry out of an untaught boy with questions alone. The puzzle has never gone away. His way through: do not ask the expert to explain. Put real cases in front of the expert, let the expert judge them, and learn from the corrections. His dissertation compared what experts said with what they did.

Why it matters now

What today's AI agents still need, he has already built

Large language models made AI fluent. Making an agent dependable inside a real organization takes what fluency cannot supply: memory of experience, the judgment of seasoned people, a model of cause and effect, hard optimization, and the discipline to act in a plant or a network without breaking it. He has built each one, in production, starting four decades ago.

The core idea · Ph.D. research, 2001, and U.S. Patent 8,326,792

What today's AI is missing: gut feel, know-how and judgment

Language models learn from what people have written down. The most valuable knowledge in an organization was never written down, because the people who hold it cannot put it into words. His life's work is the method that captures it anyway.

How it began

A B-52 brake pedal, and craftsmen who could not say what they knew

At the Air Force, his team set out to replicate the manufacture of a B-52 brake pedal. The master craftsmen helped willingly, with their time, their manuals and their answers. What they could not hand over was the tacit knowledge that made their parts come out right. Without it, the work could not be reproduced.

The software of the day failed the same way. He tried six expert-system shells, among them NASA's CLIPS, its fuzzy-logic extension and NEXPERT Object. Interviews, diaries and LISP programs captured little more than the rules already in the books. Nothing has worked very well in capturing and computerizing the experts' skills, his dissertation records.

The question took him out of computer engineering and into philosophy, back to Plato's Meno, where Socrates draws geometry out of a boy who was never taught it, and confronts the riddle of how anyone can look for knowledge they do not know they have. At NASA, Laszlo Berke, Ph.D., chief scientist for structures, put the same riddle in engineering terms: There are experts who can look at a mechanical structure or its design and pronounce whether it is an optimal design or not. How can we capture and replicate that expertise?

His answer became his Ph.D. at Case Western Reserve in 2001, under Professors Richard Boland, Yoh-Han Pao, Miles Kennedy and Fred Collopy. He filed a provisional patent application the same year, on April 30, 2001. U.S. Patent 8,326,792, "Adaptive Dynamic Personal Modeling System and Method," was granted on December 4, 2012. Its first claim protects the move at the heart of the method: the expert sees the model’s answer, enters the answer it should have given, and the model rewrites its own cause-and-effect values to match.

The gap he set out to fill

A void among the decision technologies

Every established method assumed the knowledge could be stated as data, probabilities or rules. None handled the knowledge experts act on but cannot state. His dissertation drew it as a missing piece, and set out to "map cognition onto computation, and computation back onto cognition."

The invention

Two models of one expert: what they say, and what they do

The semantic model

What the expert says

In conversation the expert names the factors that matter and scores how each cause moves each effect. The result is a working model of the rules and procedures the expert would recite.

Today: the written policy, the system prompt, the knowledge graph

vs.

The episodic model

What the expert actually did

Ten to twenty real episodes from memory, situations and outcomes, train a neural model of how the expert really decides, gut feel included.

Today: agent memory, few-shot examples, case-based reasoning

The patent also covers many experts contributing to one shared model, an "organizational memory." Laid side by side, the two reveal the gap between the rules people profess and the judgment they practice. The expert sees it, corrects the model by dragging its answers to where they should be, and the model learns. Nobody else stands between the expert and the model, and the expert owns it.

The tuner who discovered herself. Three award-winning database tuners from a Fortune 50 consulting firm built their models in under an hour each. The first had always said she tuned mainly by fixing bad SQL, the textbook approach. Her own models contained almost nothing about SQL, and she could not recall a single episode where she had relied on it. She was, the dissertation concludes, a contrarian, and the tool had given her "a window into her cognitive working style." She had simply never known how she really worked.

Proved, not assumed. The episodic model learns the parity problem (XOR), the case Minsky and Papert used to show a single-layer perceptron's limits. Its neural predictions matched the semantic model's statistics almost exactly (mean −4.08 against −4.07), showing it adds no spurious complexity, and its optimizers showed a reasonable ability to avoid getting trapped in local optima.

Method

How an expert's judgment becomes a working model

The MindModeler process, patented and used in industry. Each phase begins with a plain question to the expert.

Experts know more than they can say. Rule-based systems ask them to spell it out, and the most valuable part, their judgment, gets lost.

MindModeler works the other way. It turns an expert's qualitative judgments into a quantitative model of how they think, with their goals weighted and their constraints respected. The expert then holds a reflective dialogue with that model: asking "what if," challenging its answers, and refining it until it decides the way they would. The model keeps adapting as new experience arrives and as the organization's norms change. When the expert retires, the judgment stays.

The same idea runs through Theory Garden, his software for visual causal modeling, which helps teams make their hidden assumptions explicit when they define a problem. It was studied in published research with Richard J. Boland, Jr. at Case Western Reserve.

  1. Build the semantic model

    "What can you control, what can't you, and how does each one move the others?"

    The expert's own causes and effects become a mathematical model that can answer "what if?"

  2. Build the episodic model

    "Tell me about the times it went right, and wrong."

    Real episodes from memory refine the model and surface knowledge the expert never thought to mention.

  3. Validate and apply

    "Is this how you would decide?"

    The expert challenges the model until it holds, then it goes into procedures, training and decision tools.

The idea underneath

Mapping cognition onto computation, and back

Each way an expert thinks has a computing counterpart. MindModeler maps the expert's qualitative judgment onto those methods, computes, and maps the answer back into terms the expert can challenge.

How an expert thinksIts computing counterpart
Learning explicit relationships⟷Rule-based systems
Categorization⟷Unsupervised learning and self-organization
Memorizing and recalling objects and associations⟷Auto- and hetero-associative memories
Inference and prediction⟷Interpolation and extrapolation
Learning implicit relationships⟷Pattern recognition and exemplar-based learning

On the curse of complexity. Some problems cannot be solved exactly in any practical time. His approach recasts such a problem as an equivalent one, for example by constructing a Lyapunov function, solves that in polynomial time to a near-optimal answer, and maps the answer back. It is how he took on problems that would otherwise need impossible computing time.

A note from the inventor

Why I built MindModeler

Experts in every field decide by skill, know-how, gut feel and judgment, at operational, tactical and strategic levels. When I began this work, no method, process or system could reliably draw that knowledge out, capture it, store it and put it to use again.

My career in artificial intelligence began in 1985. Working with the U.S. Air Force, NASA, Harvard, Case Western Reserve and Fortune 500 companies, I kept meeting the same limit: the AI of the day could only follow pre-programmed "if-then" rules. What industry needed was to keep its intellectual capital, the judgment of its best people, which walks out the door when they retire and costs untold sums.

So I spent about nine years, on my own time and at my own expense, studying psychology, neuroscience, artificial intelligence, advanced mathematics, optimization and the behavioral sciences, until I had a first blueprint. Testing the theory and the software in real operations took several more years, and more than a few setbacks.

MindModeler was first released in 1997 and reached version 3.0 by 2002. Under the hood it combines clustering, data filtering and repair, statistical analysis, neural networks, evolutionary programming, simulated annealing, dynamic graphing and proprietary algorithms. The method is protected by U.S. Patent 8,326,792. It has always been a work in progress, shaped by its users, by new science and by the people who collaborate on it.

Theory Garden · since 2005

Reasoning about causal loops, without the mathematics

Managers face long chains of cause and effect that loop back on themselves. Systems-dynamics software can simulate them, but only for people who can build stocks, flows and equations. Theory Garden lets anyone draw what they believe causes what, then simulates it, with no stocks and flows and no mathematical programming.

Case Western Reserve featured it as a powerful new tool solving complex problems that require calculation skills that exceed human cognitive ability. Built with Prof. Richard J. Boland, Jr. as the visual thinking tool Theory Garden Seed, it rests on his algorithms: in the university's words, Dr. Goraya's research provides the algorithms necessary for solving those equations.

Its users have included thousands of people worldwide, more than 1,500 universities, Case Western's Executive Doctor of Management candidates, the Intelligence Advanced Research Projects Activity (IARPA) and the Department of Defense. It was applied to Afghanistan stability and counterinsurgency dynamics, the tangle of loops behind the strategy diagram the New York Times wrote about in 2010.

The invention · U.S. Patent Application 2008/0184154, with Richard J. Boland, Jr.

A drawing of your theory becomes a working simulation

Systems dynamics, the established way to simulate feedback loops, models the world as tanks and pipes: stocks, flows, and "decision valves" the user must write as "explicit mathematical equations." The application notes this takes "a high degree of sophistication," and "even a minor change in the system may require its equations to be rewritten."

Theory Garden is equationless. You draw the elements of your theory and the arrows between them, then describe each in plain qualitative terms: high or low, growing or shrinking, strong or weak, immediate or delayed, even how you feel about it. From that sparse description it builds "a robust mathematical simulation" of how every element behaves over time, so you can test the logical consequences of your own theory and compare them with what really happened.

  • Watch the theory run: links pulse and thicken as cause travels through the map, with ribbon, spiral and bar charts over time.
  • Hear it: tones and chords mark the interactions, so patterns can be heard as well as seen.
  • Brainstorm into a model: type a list of ideas, then connect them by dragging.
  • Learn across a community: saved maps cluster by structure and behavior, so people see patterns across subjects.
  • Same engine as MindModeler: a "hybrid algorithm developed to solve otherwise intractable computational problems."

It was designed for everyone from fourth graders to graduate students and professionals, to theorize about "the behavior of a chemical reaction, the dynamics of rainforest ecology, the causes of the civil war, or the experience of peer pressure," and test those theories by simulation.

Today: causal AI and world models built from human understanding rather than big data; no-code simulation; and a way for people and AI agents to test a plan's consequences, loops included, before acting on it.

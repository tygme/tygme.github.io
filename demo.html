A retiring expert's judgment, as a working model

The expert

Thirty years at one furnace, about to retire

A senior process engineer at a large mining company, with more than thirty years of experience and the option to retire. His job was to balance the emissions the EPA watches most closely, sulfur dioxide, NOx and CO, by adjusting the furnace, the primary and secondary air and the fuels. Every mine has a different ore, fuel, boiler, furnace and fan, which makes it, in the words of the 2002 project, "an art form to operate."

What the model found

Four findings the textbook would not give you

CO

The share of heat replaced, not the total heat input, is the culprit. CO climbs along an S-shaped curve as more heat is replaced.

CO

The amount of Eastern coal is not a factor in CO. More total heat input actually lowers CO, because combustion is more complete.

NOx

To reduce NOx, increase the fired pellets.

NOx and CO

To reduce both, decrease the share of heat replaced, and don't worry about total heat input, especially in the top quarter of its range.

One finding, on fuel NOx and fuel consumption, drew this note from the expert when the model showed it to him: "Ah-ha: never gave this info." The model had surfaced knowledge he had never put into words.

Test a chatbot

Ask any AI the same question

Copy this question into ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini or any other assistant. Then compare its answer with what the expert's model found.

In a low-pressure coal-burning furnace that hardens iron ore pellets, how do total heat input, the percentage of heat replaced by alternative fuel, the tonnage of fired pellets and the amount of Eastern coal each affect NOx and CO emissions? Which matters most, and how would you reduce both?
  1. Does it say the share of heat replaced, not total heat input, drives CO?
  2. Does it say Eastern coal makes no difference to CO?
  3. Does it tell you to increase fired pellets to reduce NOx?

We asked Grok the same question

A fresh chat, the question above and nothing else, no follow-ups. November 7, 2026.

The expert's model foundGrok saidMatch
CO: the share of heat replaced, not total heat input, is the culprit.CO "tracks combustion quality of the blend rather than the substitution percentage alone."Miss
CO: the amount of Eastern coal makes no difference.Eastern coal "can improve burnout (and thus lower CO)."Miss
NOx: increase fired pellets to reduce it.With more pellet tonnage, "absolute NOx and CO rise."Opposite
Both: lower the share of heat replaced; don't worry about total heat input.Total heat input "is usually the largest lever."Opposite

None of four. Two say the opposite. Grok also never mentioned primary air temperature, which the model found to be the most important control.

To be fair to Grok, its answer is sound textbook advice for pellet furnaces in general: reduce excess air, use low-NOx burners, add SNCR. That is the point. It knows what has been published about furnaces like this one. The engineer knew how this one behaved, and that was never written down. The model's findings are his judgment, built and checked with him, not laboratory measurements. One answer from one assistant is an illustration, not proof; try it yourself with the question above.

A general AI learns from what people have written. This furnace's behavior was never written down. It lived in one engineer's judgment until the model captured it. That is what the four-week sprint does.

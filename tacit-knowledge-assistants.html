Tacit Knowledge Assistants

AI that captures, and grows with, the expertise your best people cannot put into words

Tanvir Y. Goraya, Ph.D.

White paperOne-page briefBioSpoken introduction

Executive summary

Your most valuable knowledge is not in your documents, so your AI does not have it. It lives in the gut feel, know-how and judgment of a few senior people, and it leaves when they retire. Large language models and retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) learn only what was written down. A tacit knowledge assistant captures what was not: a working model of an expert’s judgment that grows with the expert and grounds your AI agents in it.

The method is not a concept. It is a 2001 Ph.D. at Case Western Reserve University and U.S. Patent 8,326,792 (provisional filed April 30, 2001; granted December 4, 2012), proven with experts from the U.S. Air Force, NASA, a Fortune 500 manufacturer and a Fortune 50 consulting firm:

  • Each expert built a working model of their own judgment in under an hour, with no knowledge engineer or programmer.
  • 74 of 74 database professionals said it would help them; 70 said no existing tool could.
  • Thirteen production database processes ran 9× faster using the experts’ captured judgment.
  • One award-winning expert discovered how she really worked, which contradicted what she had always said about her method.

The offer is simple. In four weeks, one senior expert’s judgment becomes a model the organization owns, ready to ground its AI agents and to stay when the expert retires. The question for leadership is not whether to capture that judgment, but how much of it will already be gone when you start.

The problem: what AI cannot learn from documents

Large language models and the agents built on them know what has been written. They cannot know what an organization’s best people do but never wrote down. “We can know more than we can tell,” as Michael Polanyi put it in 1966.

This knowledge has three properties that defeat today’s tools:

  1. The expert cannot state it. A decision-maker “is not necessarily aware of how he arrives at a particular decision and of his own tacit knowledge” (U.S. Patent 8,326,792). Asked how they work, experts recite the textbook.
  2. What they say differs from what they do. In the dissertation’s database study, an award-winning tuner said she relied on fixing SQL code. Her own models showed she almost never did.
  3. It changes as the expert grows. A rule base or a fine-tuned model is a snapshot. The expert keeps learning.

The usual remedies fail for the same reason. A Fortune 500 company spent three man-years building a rule-based expert system for a retiring formulation scientist; it “failed to generate acceptable custom formulations.” It then digitized his laboratory notebooks, which did “little more than to provide faster search capability.” That is exactly what document retrieval for AI does today: it finds what was written, never what was not.

The cost lands when experts retire. Their judgment walks out the door, and the AI the organization deploys knows nothing of it.

What a tacit knowledge assistant is

A tacit knowledge assistant is a working model of one expert’s judgment that the expert builds, owns and keeps refining, and that AI agents can consult. It holds two models side by side:

Semantic model Episodic model
Captures What the expert says What the expert actually did
Built from The factors that matter and how each cause moves each effect, scored in conversation 10 to 20 real episodes from memory: the situation and the outcome
Kind of model Linear, adaptive Nonlinear, exemplar-based
Today’s equivalent Written policy; a system prompt; a knowledge graph Agent memory; few-shot examples; case-based reasoning

The gap between the two models is where the tacit knowledge shows. The expert sees it, then corrects the model by dragging any wrong answer to where it should be; the model rewrites its own values to match. This is claim 1 of U.S. Patent 8,326,792.

Five properties set it apart:

  • Built by the expert, in hours. No knowledge engineer stands in between to add bias.
  • Owned by the expert. It answers the expert’s fear of being replaced, which otherwise keeps the best knowledge hidden.
  • Grows with the expert. Every correction and new episode updates it.
  • Explains itself. Visual what-if views, trend plots and 3-D surfaces show why it answers as it does.
  • Shares across experts. The patent covers many experts contributing to one “organizational memory.”

The method, proven

The method has been tested with real experts for more than twenty-five years. It began with a failure: at the U.S. Air Force, a team could not replicate the manufacture of a B-52 brake pedal. The master craftsmen helped willingly, but their tacit knowledge did not transfer. Six expert-system shells, including NASA’s CLIPS and NEXPERT Object, captured little more than the rules already in the manuals. The question led back to Plato’s Meno, where Socrates draws geometry out of a boy who was never taught it.

How it fits with today’s AI agents

The language model supplies fluency; the tacit knowledge assistant supplies the expert’s judgment, and the expert stays in the loop.

The agent consults the expert’s models before it acts, much as it would retrieve a document, but what it retrieves is judgment rather than text. Causal simulation and optimizers do the arithmetic a language model cannot do reliably. The expert keeps correcting the model and approves what matters.

Where it pays

It pays wherever results depend on a few people’s judgment, the data is sparse, and the cost of losing them is high.

How an engagement runs

The Expert Knowledge Capture engagement takes four weeks and one senior expert.

  1. Week 1. The semantic model. The expert names what they control, what they cannot, and how each factor moves the others.
  2. Week 2. Authentication. The expert tests the model with “what if” questions and drags wrong answers to where they belong.
  3. Week 3. The episodic model. Ten to twenty real episodes, the times it went right and wrong, train the model of what the expert actually does.
  4. Week 4. Validation and the path forward. The two models are compared, the gaps are discussed with the expert, and the findings go into procedures, training and AI agents.

The client keeps the models. The expert keeps ownership of their own judgment within them, which is what makes experts willing to take part.

Beyond the pilot, the same method scales to a team’s shared organizational memory and to agents grounded in it.

What we deliver

A tacit knowledge assistant: a working model of one expert’s judgment, built by the expert in hours, owned by the expert, and consulted by your AI agents.

  • Semantic model: what the expert says, as a matrix of causes and effects.
  • Episodic model: what the expert actually did, learned from 10 to 20 real episodes with a nonlinear, exemplar-based model.

  • Grounding for agents: causal simulation and constrained optimization test a plan before your agent acts.

Every organization has a few people whose judgment it cannot afford to lose. The engineer who can look at a design and know it will fail. The formulator who knows which recipe will work before the lab runs a test.

Ask them how they know, and they recite the textbook. Their real expertise was never written down. So today’s AI, which learns from what was written, never learns it either. And when they retire, it is gone.

I have spent forty years on that problem.

The answer is to model an expert twice: what they say, and what they actually do. The gap between the two is the tacit knowledge. With three award-winning database tuners, each model took under an hour, and their captured judgment made thirteen production processes nine times faster.

Today I build tacit knowledge assistants: models of your best people’s judgment, owned by them, growing with them, and grounding your AI agents.

Selected record

Systems that left the lab

  • Pishti Challenger: where it beganGraduation project · Middle East Technical University · 1985, two years early

    An AI opponent for a bluffing card game like poker. Minimax search with alpha-beta pruning chose its plays; a model of the human across the table predicted and optimized when to bluff and when to call. Every system below grew from the same three ideas: learn a model, predict, optimize.

  • Rapid Foundry Tooling Systemthe full story · U.S. Air Force Wright Laboratory · Principal Investigator · Success Story, 1995 · Aviation Week, 1995

    Automated the design of sand-casting patterns for aircraft, missile and spacecraft parts, cutting the time from two weeks to under two days, a reduction of up to 80%. In use at the San Antonio Air Logistics Center.

  • AI WARE, Inc.Founding member; Director, CIO & CTO · Acquired by Computer Associates, 1997

    A founding member alongside Prof. Yoh-Han Pao, he built on his own TG-Engine, his collection of AI tools and software. Its products reached 500+ customer installations, including Eli Lilly, Merck, Pfizer, 3M, Dow, DuPont and Procter & Gamble, and won R&D Magazine's 1994 R&D Elite Award. CA, which called itself “the world’s leading business software company,” relaunched the technology as CA Neugents.

  • NeuSight and Pegasus TechnologiesCombustion optimization for coal-fired power plants · spun off from AI WARE

    He named NeuSight and spun off Pegasus Technologies to take it to the power industry, cutting NOx and CO emissions. Shares were sold: KFx Inc. took a controlling interest in 1998, and Computer Associates later acquired AI WARE's holding.

  • CAD/Chem formulation systemAI WARE flagship · application notes, 1998 · cover story, Scientific Computing & Automation, 1992

    Learned how ingredients become product properties from sparse, noisy lab data, then searched for the best recipe under cost and process constraints. At Glidden it found an optimum outside the chemist's expected range that cut an expensive monomer from 22% to 0.9%, a 96% reduction, in about an hour. In drug-dissolution tests its held-out predictions came within 0.8%.

  • Process Advisor: a digital assistant for operatorsAI WARE · NIST-supervised die-casting project · Elsag Bailey technology partner

    Built nonlinear process models from plant data and kept updating them as equipment aged. On some 1,200 die castings it flagged 83–90% of defective parts in held-out tests. Its models ran inside Elsag Bailey controllers for real-time estimation and optimization.

  • CA Neugents: "Software That Can Think"Computer Associates, 1998–99 · IndustryWeek Technology of the Year, 1998 · the full story

    Computer Associates relaunched the technology worldwide as Neugents, short for neural agents, and built it into Unicenter TNG, its flagship, to predict server, database, application and network outages before they happened.

  • NASA: structures and experiments by AIthe full story · NASA Lewis (Glenn) Research Center · Senior Scientific Adviser to the Chief Scientist · Principal Investigator

    Combined neural networks and genetic algorithms on a CRAY X-MP to cut structural design time from months to hours.

  • MindModelerU.S. Patent 8,326,792 · sole inventor · offered through Ernst & Young's Intellinex

    Captures an expert's tacit judgment in a working model in days, with no team of programmers. Applied to emissions trade-offs (SO₂, NOₓ, CO) at one of the world's largest mining operations.

  • Ferro: color matched across 40 countries, over telephone wireSenior Scientist Level III, a vice-president-equivalent rank · Ferro Corporation, Fortune 300 · 1993–95 · the full story

    Fuzzy logic and neural networks in his lab identified paint pigments and process conditions from spectrophotometer readings sent over twisted-pair telephone lines, and sent recipes back to automotive customers in 40 countries. He also captured a retiring scientist's 50 years of tacit design judgment in a working model.

Case in point · NASA Lewis (Glenn) Research Center, 1987–1994

An AI that chose its own experiments

Every experiment in a design program costs time and money, so the question is which ones to run. In 1990 his work for NASA answered it with AI, and NASA later listed the result among its SBIR success stories, and the work went on to NASA SBIR Phase III, the commercialization stage that few awards ever reach. His one-on-one adviser and NASA contact throughout was Laszlo Berke, Ph.D., NASA Lewis's chief scientist for structures.

  • Used inside NASA in Glenn Research Center's Structural Analysis program, and at Wright-Patterson Air Force Base for composites.
  • Sold to industry as part of CAD/Chem, to customers including Eli Lilly, S.C. Johnson Wax, B.F. Goodrich, Dow Chemical and Glidden.
  • Recognized by the scientific community, NASA's report says, as "a very useful contribution" for redesigning experiments and cutting their cost.
  • Structures that design themselves. He paired neural networks with genetic algorithms on Cray supercomputers to optimize both aircraft and Space Station structures under bending, buckling and displacement limits, choosing each member's material and cross-section, choices NASA's engineers had been making by judgment. The work included structural optimization for the forward-swept wing of the X-29A, the experimental fighter flown by NASA, DARPA and the Air Force (released under FOIA; NASA technical reports server). It was part of his double master’s work, structural optimization to help the nation’s aircraft outperform the Soviets’. A foreign student at the time, he worked with the senior leadership of the Department of Defense. He also worked on energy forecasting and optimization for the Space Station.
  • The payoff. On his first day at NASA, senior leadership told him that the problem he had been given, the subject of his master’s thesis, would take more than twice the age of the universe, over 28 billion years, if every Cray X-MP worked on it and nothing else. As he recalls it, those were nearly their exact words. His neural-network method solved it in under 9 seconds on a Sun 3. A NASA Phase III success story, deployed on the Space Station (FOIA); the figures are as recorded in his career materials.

The question that started it all

There are experts who can look at a mechanical structure or its design and pronounce whether it is an optimal design or not. How can we capture and replicate that expertise?

Laszlo Berke, Ph.D., chief scientist for structures, NASA Lewis (Glenn) Research Center, as recorded in Dr. Goraya's dissertation, 2001

  • Speed was not the answer. His neural-network methods, including the Hopfield network, had already cut classical benchmark problems from 8.6 × 1050 years and 9.4 × 1098 years of computing to 33 and 109 minutes, with near-optimal results. Yet that did not answer the chief scientist's question.
  • Learning the expert instead. In 1989 at NASA Lewis he trained a neural network on a structures expert's judgment, which his dissertation records as the first use of parallel distributed processing for that purpose that his search could find. The expert was shown ten ten-bar structures, with their weight and other parameters, and gave the ten cross-sectional areas he judged optimal.
  • The result. From those ten sparse examples, the model learned the surface that spans the space of optimal solutions. He later checked the expert's answers against analytical optimization, and extended the work to a hundred-bar box-wing structure judged by an expert.
  • Where it led. The same question, how to capture what an expert sees at a glance, became his Ph.D. and U.S. Patent 8,326,792. How the method answers it.

Today: letting a model pick the most informative next experiment is called active learning or Bayesian optimization, and it is the engine of today's self-driving laboratories.

Case in point · U.S. Air Force Wright Laboratory, 1989–1998

A memory for a craft that lived in pattern makers' hands

A cast metal part starts as a pattern, and good patterns took years of apprenticeship to learn. When a pattern maker retired, that experience went too. His Air Force work gave the craft a memory.

Design News · November 19, 1990The Air Force's Rapid Design System at Wright-Patterson, the program his work grew from, remembered past designs. The magazine called that "episodal associative memory" perhaps the most advanced, ambitious, and unique aspect of RDS. The Air Force's technical lead, Maj. Steven LeClair, Ph.D.: The RDS system is self directing. The system augments humans, and, in many cases, makes decisions on its own based on rules regarding producibility, inspectability, and cost.

As Project Head and Principal Investigator, he led its successor, the Rapid Foundry Tooling System, which worked in four steps:

  1. ModelA feature-based design environment turns the blueprint into a solid model of the pattern.
  2. RememberAn episodic associative memory recalls how pattern makers handled similar parts before.
  3. DesignExpert casting knowledge adds the gates, risers, chills and parting lines.
  4. PrintStereolithography produces a physical prototype of the pattern.

The result became an Air Force Success Story: pattern design cut from two weeks to under two days, up to 80%, for complex castings in Air Force aircraft, missiles and spacecraft. It went into use at the San Antonio Air Logistics Center, and a civilian version was developed with a consortium of metal foundries at Youngstown State University.

Published by the Air Force as a Success Story, it was deployed in war theaters (FOIA). Its stereolithography prototyping supported the B-52 brake-pedal redesign at San Antonio: a design flaw was caught before manufacture, saving about $516,000 and cutting prototype time by 80%. Its design was memory-driven and feature-based, which, in his account, benefited CATIA. CATIA later designed the entire Boeing 777, “the first airplane to be 100-percent digitally designed” (Boeing, 1997), and Frank Gehry’s Peter B. Lewis Building for Case Western’s Weatherhead School (ENR, 2002). He remembers sitting in Boeing’s boardroom making the case for an enterprise information system.

Team: Air Force Wright Laboratory (lead), Case Western Reserve University, AI WARE (product and support), Wisdom Systems, XOX Corporation, the San Antonio Air Logistics Center and the Cleveland Advanced Manufacturing Program, with basic research funded by the Air Force Office of Scientific Research.

Case in point · Ferro Corporation, 1993–1995

Two kinds of expertise no one could write down

Ferro, a Fortune 300 maker of colors, coatings and specialty materials, depended on knowledge that lived in a few people's eyes and instincts. He turned both kinds into working AI. The results made him a Senior Scientist Level III, a rank equivalent to vice president, while he was still in his twenties.

Global color matching

A paint recipe, sent around the world over telephone wire

Matching an automotive paint color means working out exactly which pigments, under which processing conditions, will reproduce it. That was a job for specialists with decades of experience, studying samples under special lights.

He replaced the trained eye with a measurement and a model. An instrument at the customer's plant read the color. The readings traveled over ordinary twisted-pair telephone lines to his lab, where fuzzy logic and trained neural networks identified the pigments and processing conditions. The recipe went back around the globe on the same lines, to automotive customers in 40 countries.

  1. ReadA spectrophotometer measures the color at the customer's plant
  2. SendThe signal crosses twisted-pair telephone lines
  3. InferFuzzy logic and neural networks identify pigments and process conditions
  4. ReturnThe recipe goes back down the same wire

Today: AI delivered as a remote service, sensor to model and back, more than a decade before the cloud.

Expert knowledge capture

The scientist who knew more than he could say

A Ph.D. scientist with 50 years of experience was about to retire. He could look at a design and tell what would work, what would not, and which sensitive parameters to change. Asked how he knew, he recited the textbook. His real expertise was tacit, and it was about to walk out the door.

He captured that judgment in a working model that kept the expert's know-how in the company after he left. The project succeeded.

Today: capturing tacit expertise to ground AI agents, the problem his MindModeler patent was later built to solve.

Also at Ferro: solved an NP-complete simulation and scheduling problem.

Case in point · AI WARE, 1989–1997

One engine, three advisors

After CAD/Chem proved his neural-network and optimization engine on chemical recipes, AI WARE launched it twice more: Process Advisor for plants, and an advisor for finance and business. Each learned from history, predicted what comes next, searched for the best action, and corrected itself as new data arrived.

For chemists

CAD/Chem

Formulation modeling and optimization: learns how ingredients become properties, then finds the best recipe under cost and process constraints.

For plants

Process Advisor

Builds time-series models from historical and live plant data, finds the best setpoints and sends them to the controls, and "dynamically updates the model with on-line, real time data."

For finance and management

Business Advisor

Financial forecasting, investment optimization, risk and credit assessment, fraud detection and demand forecasting. Neural networks build "dynamic time series models"; the GESA optimizer seeks the best strategy; models update "as new data becomes available."

It learned a stock, then called the next year.

Business Advisor learned the price changes of Computer Associates' shares from October 1992 to August 1994. Its predictions then tracked the stock through September 1995, in AI WARE's words, "accurately predicted future changes."

The stock it forecast belonged to the company that went on to buy AI WARE in 1997.

Today: time-series forecasting, algorithmic trading, fraud detection, and AI agents that forecast, decide and correct themselves as the data changes.

Case in point · 1997–1999

From a Cleveland lab to "Software That Can Think"

In December 1998 the world's largest business-software company told the world it had software that could predict the future. The engine inside was the neural-network and optimization technology he had built at AI WARE.

  1. 1985–1997 · Cleveland

    AI WARE's self-teaching neural networks and genetic-algorithm optimizers were proven in "hundreds of successful design and processing support applications," in the company's words, and won R&D Magazine's R&D Elite Award in 1994.

  2. December 23, 1997 · Computer Associates acquires AI WARE

    "Neural net technology will be instrumental in addressing the next generation of business challenges," said Marc Sokol, CA's senior vice president of advanced technology.

  3. December 1998 · Launched worldwide as Neugents

    "Neural agents," built into Unicenter TNG to predict server, database, application and network outages before they occurred, and to warn of network blockages. The launch was covered in English, Chinese, Japanese, German, Dutch, Russian and Hungarian.

  4. December 1998 · IndustryWeek Technology of the Year

    The editors named Neugents one of the 25 technologies most impacting business and industry.

  5. 1999 · In the field

    "Here at Dow Chemical, we rely on CA's neural network technology to support our mission-critical chemical manufacturing operations." Jack Morris, project manager, Dow Chemical. PC Week gave Neugents "high marks" for troubleshooting at Brigham Young University.

  6. Scale

    Unicenter TNG, the product Neugents went into, was about a quarter of CA's revenue: some $1.3 billion of $5.25 billion in fiscal 1999, according to CA's annual report to the SEC.
